The Problem

Modernization has been broken for too long.

Bank technology leaders are asked to deliver continuous innovation on platforms designed for an era of episodic change. The two prevailing answers, replacing the core or bolting on point solutions, both fail in predictable ways. Programs overrun. Architectures fragment. Risk accumulates faster than value is delivered.

Big-bang transformation

Multi-year core replacements promise a clean slate but routinely overrun by years and over-budget by hundreds of millions. The longer the program runs, the further the destination drifts from the bank that commissioned it. Few finish. Fewer succeed.

Point solutions

The opposite extreme, a fintech for every gap, produces short-term wins and long-term entropy. Each integration ages independently, contracts proliferate, the canonical model fragments, and operations inherit a permanent integration tax that grows year over year.

The FinX Approach

A different model. Continuous evolution, not episodic transformation.

FinX is not a product line. It is a delivery model: a BIAN-aligned orchestration layer, an iterative engagement pattern, and a coexistence strategy that keeps the existing core productive while new capability is composed around it.

Why BIAN

Why BIAN provides the right framework.

BIAN, the Banking Industry Architecture Network, is a vendor-neutral, industry-standard reference model for banking capabilities. It provides three properties no proprietary architecture can match.

Property 01

Vendor-neutral foundation

BIAN is owned by the industry, not by a vendor. Banks that align to BIAN avoid lock-in to a single core, fintech, or integrator. Components can be swapped at the seams that BIAN defines, not at seams a vendor invented.

Property 02

Common language

Architects, vendors, and regulators converge on the same vocabulary. A "Party Lifecycle Management" service domain means the same thing in a Tier 1 bank, a fintech, and a regulator. That common language is what makes meaningful composability possible.

Property 03

Incremental change

BIAN service domains are independently addressable. A bank can modernize Payments without touching Lending, or replace a single fintech connector without rewriting consumers. Big-bang programs become unnecessary because the architecture supports surgical change.

Coexistence

Coexistence and risk reduction.

The decisive advantage of a BIAN-aligned approach is that the bank does not have to choose between modernization and stability. Old and new run side by side, addressed through the same canonical model.

Without a structured approach

A patchwork that never coheres.

Point integrations multiply. Each new fintech adds another bespoke seam. Channels carry orchestration logic that should live in the platform. Every release becomes a regression-test exercise across the whole estate, and every modernization step is treated as a risk to the existing book of business.

With BIAN-aligned coexistence

Old and new addressed through one canonical model.

Legacy systems are wrapped behind BIAN service domains. New capabilities are added against the same surface. A migration is a switch behind the canonical contract. Channels, partners, and downstream systems do not need to know which side is serving the request. Risk is contained to the seam being changed.

Data & AI

Data, AI, and governance.

Composable architecture is also the precondition for trustworthy AI. The same canonical model that lets a bank swap a connector lets it govern the data feeding decisions.

Data

A canonical data spine

Events from every BIAN domain feed into the FinX Data Lake on Microsoft Fabric. Lineage, retention, and access controls are platform concerns, not project concerns.

AI

Governed AI surfaces

AI capabilities, including onboarding decisioning, alert triage, and narrative generation, sit on top of the canonical model with explicit human-in-the-loop steps and full audit. Roadmap items are clearly marked as such.

Governance

Auditability by construction

Every workflow step, every API call, every operator action carries an immutable trail. Regulators and auditors get a single view; banks get evidence that the platform is enforceable, not just configurable.

Innovation

Innovation within a structured environment.

BIAN defines clear boundaries between capabilities. That structure is what allows new products, services, and integrations to be introduced without disrupting existing functionality. Rather than periodic transformation programmes, new capabilities can be introduced continuously, with each addition strengthening the overall platform.

Speed

Structured boundaries enable speed

Clear service domain interfaces mean new capabilities can be introduced, tested, and deployed without disrupting existing operations, dramatically shortening time to market for new products and features.

Scale

Consistency at scale

Standard data models and APIs ensure that each new addition remains coherent with the broader platform. Innovation compounds rather than fragments. Technical debt does not accumulate at the seams.

Ecosystem

Ecosystem integration

BIAN-aligned interfaces simplify integration with third-party fintechs, data providers, and regulatory systems, expanding the innovation surface without increasing architectural risk or creating new bespoke seams.

Resilience

Competitive resilience

The ability to respond to market change rapidly and confidently, without accumulated technical debt, becomes a durable structural advantage over institutions operating on less structured, monolithic foundations.

Path · Coexistence~9 months to first new capability

Wrap the legacy. Add the new.

Most banks do not have the option to start over. The coexistence path puts BIAN-aligned service domains in front of existing cores so new capabilities can be added without re-platforming. Channels, partners, and Glass speak only to the canonical contract; what sits behind it is an implementation detail.

Best for

Incumbent banks who can't replace the legacy core today.

Banks where the existing core runs the book of business, where regulatory and operational continuity are non-negotiable, and where a multi-year core replacement is not on the table. The coexistence path delivers new capability in months while leaving the underlying core in place.

Without vs With

Two ways to add capability to a legacy estate.

Without a structured approach

Point-to-point integration

  • Every new channel and partner integrates directly to the legacy core
  • Customer and product data fragments across systems
  • Each new capability adds to integration debt rather than reducing it
  • Compliance and audit trails are reconstructed system by system
  • Future core replacement gets harder, not easier, with every release
With BIAN-aligned coexistence

Canonical contract in front

  • Channels, Glass, and partners integrate only to the canonical BIAN contract
  • Glue mediates between the contract and the legacy core
  • New capability can ship behind the contract without touching the core
  • One canonical event stream feeds compliance, audit, and analytics
  • Future replacement of the core becomes a swap behind the seam, not a rebuild

Migration path

From coexistence to progressive.

The coexistence configuration is also the foundation of the progressive path. Once the canonical contract is in place and channels speak only to it, individual BIAN domains can be migrated off the legacy core one at a time. Each migration is contained behind the seam.

Step 01
Wrap
Glue exposes BIAN domains in front of the legacy core. Channels move to the canonical contract.
Step 02
Augment
New capability, including IDV, AML, document directory, and Glass, ships behind the contract without touching the core.
Step 03
Decouple
Domain by domain, the canonical contract becomes the source of truth. Legacy moves toward systems-of-record only.
Step 04
Replace
Optional. With the seam in place, individual domains can be migrated off legacy under the progressive path.

Migrate one domain at a time.

A multi-year program with continuous value delivery and reversible checkpoints. Each BIAN service domain is migrated behind the canonical contract; channels, partners, and Glass stay stable throughout. There is no big-bang cutover.

Best for

Banks ready to migrate gradually over 18 to 36 months.

Banks who already have a coexistence seam in place, or are willing to put one in as Step 1, and want to retire legacy domain by domain rather than all at once. The progressive path delivers the long-run value of full modernization without the delivery risk of a big-bang program.

Capability migration order

A typical sequencing.

The order below is the default UST recommendation. It moves the lowest-risk, highest-control domains first, leaving the book of business on the legacy core until late in the program.

Wave 02

Wave 03

Wave 04
Payments

Decommissioning checkpoints

Legacy retires only after the seam holds.

Checkpoint 01 · Shadow

New domain runs in shadow behind the canonical contract for an agreed period. All traffic still served by legacy; FinX results compared offline.

Checkpoint 02 · Cutover

FinX becomes primary for new traffic. Legacy still authoritative for historical data. Reversible by routing change at the seam.

Checkpoint 03 · Decommission

Legacy domain is read-only, then archived. Decommissioning is gated on a clean shadow period and a successful audit cycle.

Risk gates

No wave starts until the previous wave is green.

  • Functional parity gate: canonical contract behavior matches legacy for the in-scope domain across the agreed test corpus.
  • Operational gate: Glass operators have run the domain in production for a full SLA window with no Sev-1 incidents.
  • Compliance gate: audit trail, regulatory export, and four-eyes controls are demonstrably equivalent or stronger than legacy.
  • Reversibility gate: a documented and rehearsed routing change at the seam returns traffic to legacy within an agreed RTO.
  • Steering gate: bank, UST, and partner steering jointly sign off before the next wave begins.

Frequently asked questions

Common questions from evaluators and architects.

Is FinX a core banking system?
No. FinX Glue is an orchestration and integration layer, and FinX Glass is an operations console. Core banking, including ledger, accounts, and payments execution, is provided by partners such as Thought Machine Vault Core, sitting behind the canonical contract.
Why BIAN, specifically?
BIAN gives the platform a vendor-neutral foundation, a common language across the bank and its partners, and a model that supports incremental change. The white paper covers this in detail in Section 02.
Do banks have to replace their core to use FinX?
No. The coexistence path keeps the legacy core in place and puts the canonical contract in front of it. Most incumbent banks start there.
How long does a typical engagement take to first value?
Greenfield engagements typically reach first live customer in around six months. Coexistence engagements typically reach first new capability in around nine months. Progressive modernization is an 18 to 36 month program delivered in waves, with continuous value at each wave.
What does the canonical contract cover today?
Party Lifecycle Management, Document Directory, IDV/AML orchestration, Account Management, and Payments. The contract is BIAN-aligned and extensible.
Which fintech connectors are supported out of the box?
Jumio for IDV, ComplyAdvantage for AML and sanctions, Zoot for decisioning, Microsoft Dynamics 365 for customer engagement, Microsoft Fabric for analytics, Wolters Kluwer for regulatory reporting, Oracle NetSuite for finance, Formpipe and Thirdstream for documents and onboarding.
How is regulatory compliance handled across coexistence?
Every action against the canonical contract emits a canonical event with actor, role, rationale, and before/after state. Regulatory export reads the canonical event store. Coverage extends as more domains migrate behind the contract.
Can banks extend the canonical model?
Yes, through a controlled extensibility model documented in the Glue Developer Hub. Extensions are namespaced so they do not conflict with the canonical surface or with future platform releases.
How is data and AI handled?
Canonical events stream into Microsoft Fabric. The canonical model is the data model. AI initiatives build against governed datasets with lineage and access control inherited from Fabric.
How do I get a sandbox tenant?
Reach out via the contact details below or through your UST account team. Sandbox tenants are provisioned with synthetic data and deterministic IDV/AML connector responses for end-to-end testing.
What is the relationship between UST and FinX?
FinX is a product venture of UST, a global digital transformation company with more than 33,000 employees and over $2 billion in annual revenue. UST brings deep experience in financial services, large-scale delivery capability, and managed services expertise. FinX is the product layer; UST provides the delivery and managed services capability.
How does FinX support data and AI initiatives?
Canonical events from every domain stream into Microsoft Fabric. The canonical model is the data model, so every analytics and AI initiative builds on a stable, semantically consistent foundation. AI capabilities can be embedded directly into business processes, such as onboarding decisioning and alert triage, rather than operating as standalone tools. All AI steps include human-in-the-loop review and full audit.
What makes FinX different from a fintech-led modernization approach?
A fintech-led approach can accelerate innovation in specific areas but often introduces complexity and fragmentation. Without a coherent architectural framework, each point solution adds integration burden and creates new dependencies. FinX introduces a structured orchestration layer that gives each fintech connector a defined, canonical position. New connectors plug in without creating new bespoke seams across the estate.

Glossary

The full A-Z reference for FinX platform, BIAN, architecture, integration, and governance terminology. Definitions are sourced from FinX engineering and product documentation.

A

Adapter

A provider connector that maps canonical (BIAN) payloads to target systems (cores, fintechs, CRM) and protocols, enabling independent evolution. One adapter per target system. Adapters execute a five-step pipeline: read target config, transform request, call target API, handle response, transform response. Governed by the canonical model, with no direct service-domain-to-system coupling. Two primary adapters today: finx-glue-cif-adapter-service (target: FinX-CIF) and finx-glue-tm-account-adapter-service (target: TM Vault Core).

Source: BIAN Glossary and Standards (FinX Context); FinX Glue Architecture; FinX Glue Services and Adapter details.

B

Behavior Qualifier (BQ)

A subdivision of a Control Record describing a specific aspect or behavior (for example KYC, Statements, Amount Block). BQs appear as nested resources and operations in APIs. FinX may add sub-qualifiers while keeping alignment to the parent BQ semantics.

Source: BIAN Glossary and Standards (FinX Context).

BIAN API

A canonical, service-domain-scoped business API that adheres to BIAN semantics using the BIAN generic operation verbs. The full set of BIAN v14 generic operations is: initiate (create a new service-domain instance), retrieve (return details), update (modify), record (capture information), execute (perform an activity), evaluate (assess or score), control (apply a control action), register (register a new entry), notify (send a state-change notification), request (make a formal request), grant (authorise access or permission), provide (supply information on demand), feedback (submit review or outcome), and exchange (two-way data exchange). Not every operation applies to every service domain. The applicable operations are determined by the service domain's Functional Pattern. FinX Glue exposes initiate, retrieve, update, record, execute, evaluate, control, register, and notify across its published Glue service APIs. BIAN APIs are the contract of record in FinX. Platform and tenant variability is expressed via governed extensions, not API shape changes.

Source: BIAN Glossary and Standards (FinX Context).

BIAN BOM (Business Object Model)

The canonical, BIAN-aligned business information model that standardizes attributes and relationships across service domains. The BOM drives canonical schemas in the Schema Registry and powers transformations between canonical payloads and target systems. All BIAN implementations must treat the BOM as the authoritative source for data definitions, relationships, and constraints (ADR-FINX-CIF-001).

Source: BIAN Glossary and Standards (FinX Context); ADR, FinX CIF.

BIAN Business Scenario

A BIAN-defined end-to-end transaction pattern that sequences interactions across multiple service domains to accomplish a specific business goal. Business Scenarios serve as blueprints for the FinX Business Abstraction Layer. Key onboarding-relevant scenarios include: Retail Customer Onboarding (Party Reference Data Directory → Know Your Customer → Customer Agreement → Savings Account); Account Opening (Customer Product and Service Directory → Current Account or Savings Account → Position Keeping); KYC Refresh (Know Your Customer → Party Reference Data Directory update). FinX Business APIs (target state) expose these multi-service-domain sequences as single callable endpoints.

Source: BIAN v14 Specification.

BIAN Landscape

The complete taxonomy of approximately 325 BIAN service domains organized into 20 Business Areas that together cover the full operational scope of a bank. Business Areas include Financial Institutions, Sales and Service, Operations and Execution, Risk and Compliance, Business Direction and Support, and others. FinX Glue implements a targeted subset of service domains relevant to onboarding, account management, and payments. The scope is extended per client engagement by adding BIAN-aligned Glue services for additional service domains. The Landscape is the primary reference for identifying which service domain owns a given capability when scoping new integrations.

Source: BIAN v14 Specification.

BIAN Team

Owns specification domain knowledge, drives schema customizations, and raises PRs for schema changes. Responsible for identifying service-domain candidates, downloading base BIAN specs, and customizing on feature branches.

Source: Schema Registry and SoP for onboarding new BIAN services in Glue.

Business Abstraction Layer (BAL)

The new architectural layer being introduced on top of the existing BIAN Service APIs and Adapter Framework. It exposes business capabilities, specifically Account Opening and Customer Onboarding, as single-call Business APIs. Responsibilities: orchestration, policy enforcement, state management, and unified response. Today, channels must orchestrate multiple BIAN API calls per journey. In the target state, channels call one Business API and FinX Glue handles all orchestration internally. BIAN APIs remain intact and are used internally.

Source: Transitioning FinX Glue to a Business Capability Platform, current and target states.

C

Canonical Model

The unified, BIAN-aligned representation of business data used across APIs, events, and transformations to avoid point-to-point models. Realized through the BIAN BOM and enforced by the Schema Registry and CI policies.

Source: BIAN Glossary and Standards (FinX Context).

CIF (Customer Information File)

The FinX-CIF application and database that serves as the customer master data store. The finx-glue-cif-adapter-service integrates with FinX-CIF for customer creation, customer search, and customer updates. It also maintains Document Directory data, Customer Agreement data, and Customer Product Services data in the FinX-CIF database as different collections. CIF is the adapter target for Party Reference Data Directory, Customer Product & Service Directory, Customer Agreement, and Document Directory service domains. ADR decisions (ADR-FINX-CIF-001 through ADR-FINX-CIF-006) govern how the BIAN BOM maps to CIF data models.

Source: FinX Glue Services and Adapter details; ADR, FinX CIF.

ComplyAdvantage (CA) / Mesh

The KYC and AML screening provider integrated through a FinX adapter into the ComplyAdvantage Mesh API. Supports individual, corporate, and beneficiary entity screening. Uses a create-and-screen synchronous workflow with webhook callbacks (CASE_CREATED, CASE_STATE_UPDATED, WORKFLOW_COMPLETED). Screens against OFAC, sanctions lists, PEPs, and adverse media. Transaction screening is handled by a separate service. Webhook subscription is performed manually by contacting ComplyAdvantage support.

Source: FinX Microservices Details.

Conductor OSS

The orchestration backbone of FinX Glue, enhanced to support BPMN-compliant workflow definitions (JSON or YAML). Orchestrates microservices, APIs, events, human tasks, and UI steps. Supports process states such as wait, resume, parallelization, branching, timers, retries, and escalations. Note: per TRD §5.4, orchestration is currently hand-coded in microservices. Conductor-driven runtime is the target state.

Source: Process & Orchestration; FinX Glue Testing Requirements for QA and Test Team.

Control Record (CR)

The primary business object managed by a service domain and the focal point for operations (for example Customer, Current Account). Control Records define top-level resources in OpenAPI specs and anchor persistence, orchestration, and policy tagging.

Source: BIAN Glossary and Standards (FinX Context).

D

DEMO

A next-generation banking integration and interoperability platform: a BIAN-aligned, cloud-native framework that accelerates digital transformation for banks and fintechs. Functional Pattern

The structural archetype assigned to each BIAN service domain that determines its behavior and the set of applicable generic operations. BIAN v14 defines the following core patterns used in FinX:

  • Fulfill: manages the complete product or service lifecycle for a customer (for example Current Account, Savings Account, Term Deposit). Supports initiate, retrieve, update, execute, and record.
  • Administer: manages reference or configuration data with long-term persistence (for example Party Reference Data Directory, Customer Agreement, Document Directory, Customer Product and Service Directory). Supports initiate, retrieve, update, register, and record.
  • Process: orchestrates a complex, multi-step business process to a conclusion (for example Know Your Customer). Supports initiate, retrieve, update, record, and execute.
  • Monitor: observes and reports on a business state or position (for example Position Keeping). Supports retrieve, update, record, and notify.
  • Transact: executes a discrete, atomic business action with immediate completion (for example Payment Order). Supports initiate, retrieve, and record.

Knowing the Functional Pattern of a service domain tells engineers which BIAN operations to expect and helps partners predict API shape before consulting the OpenAPI spec.

Source: BIAN v14 Specification.

Thesis

The thesis in one paragraph.

Banking modernization has spent two decades oscillating between two failures: incremental upgrades that never reach the new estate, and big-bang core replacements that consume capital and political tolerance for years before they deliver value. The structural answer is a BIAN-aligned canonical contract that lets a bank wrap what is, add what is needed, and migrate at the seams the architecture defines, not the seams a vendor invented. That is what FinX is.

Strategic advantages

Four strategic advantages.

Each one is a property of the architecture, not a marketing claim. The white paper builds the case in full; the summary is here.

Advantage 01

Interoperability without lock-in.

Legacy cores, modern core platforms, and fintech connectors all sit behind the same canonical model. Components can be swapped at the seams BIAN defines. A bank is never committed to a single core, a single fintech, or a single integrator for the next decade.

Advantage 02

Governance and compliance by design.

Policy-as-code, segregation of duties, service identity, lineage, consent, and runtime validation are platform properties. Alignment to PCI-DSS, GDPR, MAS TRM, and ISO 27001 is built into the canonical contract, not bolted on per project.

Advantage 03

Composable banking experiences.

BIAN-aligned APIs, schema-driven UI flows, and orchestrated journeys let the bank assemble new products in weeks rather than redevelop them in quarters. The same canonical surface serves channels, partners, and the operations console.

Advantage 04

Orchestration as a first-class layer.

APIs, events, human tasks, rules, and UI steps are coordinated by the platform, not by the channel. A change to a journey is a change to one orchestration, not a release across every channel that consumes it.

Strategic direction

From integration platform to business capability platform.

FinX Glue began as a BIAN-aligned integration layer. The next step is the Business Abstraction Layer.

Today

Channels orchestrate the journey.

A retail onboarding flow today sequences a series of BIAN API calls from the channel: Party Reference Data, Know Your Customer, Customer Agreement, Savings Account. Each channel carries the orchestration logic. Each new channel repeats it.

Target

The platform exposes the journey.

The Business Abstraction Layer publishes Business APIs such as Customer Onboarding and Account Opening as single calls. Orchestration, retries, compensation, policy enforcement, and unified responses move into the platform. Channels become thinner. New channels are days, not quarters.

Risk

The risks worth naming.

The risks that matter to an executive sponsor are not the day-to-day delivery risks. They are the structural risks that decide whether the platform holds its shape under load and change.

  • BIAN contract drift. Breaking changes to the canonical contract cascade into every consumer. Mitigation: contract testing in CI, schema-registry review, backward-compatibility gates on every release.
  • Adapter instability. Third-party platforms (cores, IDV, AML, e-signature) change behavior unilaterally. Mitigation: adapter-specific regression packs, controlled stubs, environment isolation.
  • Performance discovered late. Production load patterns are different from test patterns. Mitigation: a dedicated performance environment, performance gates at every promotion to the customer-facing demo environment.
  • Demo treated as release-ready. A clean demo is not a clean release. Mitigation: mandatory smoke and regression subsets, ops-readiness checklist, no go-live without it.
  • Security misconfiguration. A misconfigured gateway or policy is the failure mode that matters most. Mitigation: automated security checks in CI, role-based and attribute-based access control suites, periodic external review.

Key concepts

Three concepts underpin how Glue works. Understanding them makes every API call predictable.

Concept 01

The canonical contract

Every channel, connector, and Glass module talks to one stable API surface. The underlying implementation, which core, which fintech connector, can change without breaking consumers. The contract is what stays fixed.

Concept 02

BIAN service domains

Each API surface maps to a BIAN service domain: Party Lifecycle Management, Account Management, Payments, Document Directory, Customer Case. The domain defines the semantics; Glue provides the implementation and the routing.

Every request carries an X-Tenant header. All data, events, and configuration are fully isolated per tenant. Sandbox, integration, and production tenants run on the same Glue infrastructure with no cross-tenant data access.

Quickstart

Get from zero to a working Glue integration in under thirty minutes. This guide walks through tenant provisioning, authentication, your first API call, and verifying webhook delivery. By the end you will have created a party, attached a document, and seen the lifecycle event arrive in your webhook.

Before you start

  • A FinX sandbox tenant (request via your UST account team)
  • An OAuth2 client ID and secret
  • A webhook endpoint reachable from the sandbox
  • curl or your HTTP client of choice

Step 1: Authenticate

Glue uses OAuth2 client credentials. Exchange your client ID and secret for a bearer token at the sandbox token endpoint. Tokens are tenant-scoped and expire in one hour.

cURL
curl -X POST https://auth.sandbox.ustfinx.com/oauth2/token \
  -H "Content-Type: application/x-www-form-urlencoded" \
  -d "grant_type=client_credentials" \
  -d "client_id=$CLIENT_ID" \
  -d "client_secret=$CLIENT_SECRET" \
  -d "scope=glue:read glue:write"
Token scope. Request only the scopes your integration needs. Sandbox tokens are valid for 3600 seconds. Rotate them before expiry rather than waiting for a 401.

Step 2: Set tenant context

Every request requires two headers beyond Authorization: an X-Tenant header that scopes the request to your sandbox tenant, and an Idempotency-Key that makes write operations safe to retry.

Headers
Authorization: Bearer <access_token>
X-Tenant: your-sandbox-tenant-id
Idempotency-Key: <uuid-v4>
Content-Type: application/json

Step 3: Initiate a party

Call POST /v1/parties/initiate to create a new party record in the pending_kyc lifecycle state. The response includes a partyId you will use in subsequent calls. See the API Reference for the full parameter list.

cURL · Request
curl -X POST https://api.sandbox.ustfinx.com/v1/parties/initiate \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" \
  -H "X-Tenant: your-sandbox-tenant-id" \
  -H "Idempotency-Key: $(uuidgen)" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{
    "type": "individual",
    "givenName": "Sample",
    "familyName": "Customer",
    "dateOfBirth": "1990-04-12",
    "residency": "GB"
  }'
Response · 201 Created
{
  "partyId": "pty_01HZ5KGQR4BVMX9W3TJNF7CAE",
  "type": "individual",
  "status": "pending_kyc",
  "createdAt": "2026-04-29T09:14:33Z",
  "tenant": "your-sandbox-tenant-id"
}

Step 4: Attach a document

Use the partyId returned in Step 3 to attach an identity document via POST /v1/documents. The Document Directory stores the reference and makes it available to IDV and AML connectors. Supported document types include passport, driving_license, and national_id. Provide metadata and a secure reference URL; the platform stores the link, not the binary.

cURL
curl -X POST https://api.sandbox.ustfinx.com/v1/documents \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" \
  -H "X-Tenant: your-sandbox-tenant-id" \
  -H "Idempotency-Key: $(uuidgen)" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{
    "partyId": "pty_01HZ5KGQR4BVMX9W3TJNF7CAE",
    "documentType": "passport",
    "issuingCountry": "GB",
    "expiryDate": "2031-03-20",
    "referenceUrl": "https://secure-store.example.com/docs/abc123"
  }'

Step 5: Verify your webhook

As the party moves through lifecycle states, Glue emits canonical events to your registered webhook endpoint. Within seconds of the POST /v1/parties/initiate call you should receive a party.created event at your endpoint.

Webhook payload
{
  "eventId": "evt_01HZ5KHMNP2QWRX8V6FYJD93B",
  "eventType": "party.created",
  "occurredAt": "2026-04-29T09:14:33Z",
  "tenant": "your-sandbox-tenant-id",
  "data": {
    "partyId": "pty_01HZ5KGQR4BVMX9W3TJNF7CAE",
    "type": "individual",
    "status": "pending_kyc"
  }
}
Signature verification. Every webhook delivery includes an X-FinX-Signature header containing an HMAC-SHA256 digest of the raw request body, signed with your webhook secret. Always verify this signature before processing the event.

Authentication and tenant headers

Glue uses OAuth2 client credentials with tenant-scoped tokens. This page covers the full authentication flow, the required headers on every request, and how multi-tenant isolation works at the API surface.

OAuth2 client credentials

Every Glue integration is backed by a registered OAuth2 client. The client exchanges its ID and secret for a short-lived access token at the tenant token endpoint, then sends that token on every API call.

cURL
curl -X POST https://auth.sandbox.ustfinx.com/oauth2/token \
  -H "Content-Type: application/x-www-form-urlencoded" \
  -d "grant_type=client_credentials" \
  -d "client_id=$CLIENT_ID" \
  -d "client_secret=$CLIENT_SECRET" \
  -d "scope=glue:read glue:write"

Token scope and expiry

Access tokens are valid for one hour. Request only the scopes you need: glue:read for read-only callers, glue:write for write-capable integrations, plus domain-specific scopes such as glue:payments when relevant.

Refresh proactively by exchanging client credentials again before the existing token expires. Never wait for a 401 in production: the round trip costs the customer-facing latency budget.

Caching guidance. Cache access tokens at the integration layer with a TTL of 50 minutes. If your service is multi-instance, share the cache through Redis or an equivalent so you do not mint a token per pod.

Required headers on every request

  • Authorization: Bearer <token>: The access token from the OAuth2 exchange.
  • X-Tenant: <tenant-id>: Identifies which bank tenant the request applies to. Required even when the token is single-tenant, for explicit isolation.
  • Idempotency-Key: <uuid>: Required on all writes. Glue stores the response for 24 hours so retries return the original result without duplicating side effects.
  • Content-Type: application/json: All request and response bodies are JSON.

Multi-tenant isolation

Glue is built for multi-tenant operation. Tokens, data, configuration, connectors, and audit trails are all scoped to a tenant. Cross-tenant reads are not possible through the canonical API surface.

What crosses the tenant boundary: platform release notes, shared connector versions, the BIAN canonical model itself. What does not: any customer data, party records, account records, document references, audit events, or operator activity.

Errors and recovery

  • 401 Unauthorized: Token missing, malformed, or expired. Refresh and retry.
  • 403 Forbidden: Token is valid but lacks the required scope, or the X-Tenant header does not match the token's tenant. Check scope grants in client registration.
  • 409 Conflict (idempotency): A request with the same Idempotency-Key has already completed with a different body. Generate a new key for genuinely new requests; reuse the original key for safe retries.
  • 429 Too Many Requests: Tenant rate limit hit. Honour the Retry-After header.

Environments

FinX Glue runs in three environments: sandbox, integration, and production. Each has different data semantics, connector behavior, and rate limits. Pick the right environment for the work you are doing.

Sandbox

Synthetic data only. IDV and AML connectors return deterministic outcomes keyed off the input payload, so end-to-end testing is reproducible without depending on third-party fintechs. No customer data, no real money movement, no production traffic. Free for any registered partner. The right place for early development, exploration, and CI integration tests.

Integration

Shared environment for partner testing across UST and bank teams. Anonymised data with realistic scale and shape. Near-production rate limits. Connectors point at vendor sandboxes (Jumio sandbox, ComplyAdvantage test, and so on). Ideal for partner integration testing, pre-UAT smoke runs, and performance shake-out.

Pre-production

Mirrors production architecturally. Tenant-isolated per bank. Used for user acceptance testing, soak testing, and dry-run cutover rehearsals. Connectors can be configured to point at production fintech endpoints with throttled traffic, or to vendor staging environments.

Production

Real customer data, real money movement. Audit-tracked, regulated, and operated under the bank's compliance regime. Production access is gated and provisioned per tenant by UST and the bank's platform team. Production tokens cannot be minted from sandbox client registrations; environments are fully separated at the auth boundary.

Promotion path

Code, configuration, and connector versions move through the environments in the same order: sandbox to integration to pre-production to production. Each promotion is a deliberate step: artefacts are versioned, signed, and tracked. There is no automatic promotion from sandbox to production; pre-production is always the gate before production release.

Customer onboarding and KYC journey

7 journey stepsPLM · IDV · AML · TMBIAN v14 aligned

This guide is the canonical reference for the FinX retail savings onboarding journey, from product selection to a funded, active account. It walks through the seven steps in sequence, names the BIAN service domains that own each interaction, and calls out where the platform delegates to fintech connectors. Use it when mapping FinX onto an existing bank journey, or when designing a channel that composes against the Glue contract directly.

Scope. The reference journey described here is retail savings account opening. Other onboarding journeys (corporate, joint, minor, lending) reuse the same service domains and orchestration patterns but vary in the steps that apply. Channel and Glass implementations should follow the seven-step structure as a baseline, then add or remove steps per product.

Async behaviour. Steps 3 and 4 are asynchronous from the channel's perspective. IDV typically resolves in 10 to 15 seconds; AML screening completes synchronously within the workflow but emits webhook events for downstream consumers. Never block a customer-facing thread on a synchronous IDV or AML result. Poll the lifecycle status endpoint or subscribe to the corresponding webhook.

Step 1: Prospect qualification

The customer lands on the channel's Product Selection screen and chooses an account type, product, currency, and intended use of account. All dropdown values are data-driven from Glue master APIs and can be changed without a code deployment. No customer information is captured on this screen and no external service call is made; the Enroll button enables only when all mandatory fields contain valid data.

  • Fail (non-recoverable). None at this step.

The journey moves through two screens. The first captures email and mobile number; on blur, each field triggers a de-duplication check against the Customer Master. If a duplicate is found, the channel shows a configurable message inviting the customer to sign in to their existing portal. If no duplicate is found, an Application ID is issued and the customer enters name and date of birth on the Personal Details screen.

  • Fail (recoverable). Duplicate detected. Customer routed to sign-in flow; may restart with a different identifier.
  • Fail (non-recoverable). None at this step in the reference implementation. OTP and email verification are out of scope for the reference journey and are added per tenant.

The customer enters identity document details (type, number, issuing authority, issue and expiry dates) and uploads front and back images. A consent checkbox is a hard prerequisite. Data is captured on the Identification Details screen but the IDV check itself runs asynchronously during data validation, after the customer submits the application on the Preview screen. The customer sees the IDV outcome only on the final Application Decision screen.

Step 4: KYC and AML screening

KYC and AML screening is a fully back-end step. The customer does not see a dedicated screen; screening runs during data validation immediately after submission. The full customer profile assembled in Steps 2 and 3 is sent to the screening connector. The decision is the gate before account creation: a clear result proceeds silently, matches or elevated risk are surfaced to the compliance review queue, and confirmed high risk halts the journey.

Inputs

The screening call uses the data already collected: name, gender, date and country of birth, nationality, address (residential and mailing), contact information, and identity document details. No additional fields are captured at this step.

Behind the scenes

The Know Your Customer service domain orchestrates a synchronous create-and-screen workflow against the AML connector. The workflow runs five sequential steps: customer creation, initial risk scoring, screening against sanctions, PEP and adverse media lists, alert generation, and case creation. The connector returns two response fields, an overall risk level and a screening result, which combine into the decision in the lookup table below. Three webhook events (case created, case state updated, workflow completed) are published per environment for downstream consumers.

  • Fail (non-recoverable). REJECTED ends the journey with a configurable customer-facing message.

Step 5: Account creation

Account creation runs in the back end after a positive Application Decision. There is no customer screen during this step; the customer sees the Congratulations page next, with their Customer ID and Account Number displayed. The flow uses a deliberately staged pattern: the account is created in a pending state, parameters are applied, and only then is the account activated. This gives the platform a natural compensation boundary if a downstream step fails.

Sub-flow

  1. Create the party. A customer (party) record is created in Thought Machine Vault from the profile assembled in Steps 2 to 4.
  2. Open the account in pending state. POST /v2/accounts with ACCOUNT_STATUS_PENDING. Parameters are not supplied at this point.
  3. Apply parameters. POST /v1/parameter-values:batchCreate against the new account, applying the product's parameter set.
  4. Activate. PUT /v2/accounts/{accountId} transitions the account from ACCOUNT_STATUS_PENDING to ACCOUNT_STATUS_OPEN.
  5. Record consent. Three sequential calls under Customer Agreement create the master consent record, store the consent value alongside the Application ID and Party ID, then update the party lifecycle status to completed.
  6. Register the product instance. The new account is registered in Customer Product and Service Directory so it appears in subsequent product lookups for this customer.

Behind the scenes

The Savings Account BIAN service domain routes through the TM Vault adapter. The same staged pattern (create pending, configure, activate) is used for Current Account and other Fulfill-pattern products; only the product reference and parameter set change.

Exit conditions

  • Pass. Account reaches ACCOUNT_STATUS_OPEN, consent recorded, product registered. Customer proceeds to Step 7 (Step 6 applies only where DocuSign is wired in).
  • Fail (recoverable). If a downstream sub-step fails, the account can remain in pending state. The reference architecture compensates via the saga model described below.
  • Fail (non-recoverable). Complete failure of party or account creation ends the journey at the Application Decision stage; no partial account is created.

Step 6: Agreement signing

Step 6 covers e-signature for products that require a counter-signed legal agreement, typically corporate onboarding, lending, and joint accounts. The retail savings reference journey records consent inline at Step 5 (Customer Agreement) and does not invoke the e-signature connector. Where Step 6 applies, the channel sends a pre-populated agreement template to the recipient and polls the envelope status endpoint until the document is signed and stored.

Reference scope. The retail savings reference journey skips Step 6. The DocuSign integration is a platform capability available to tenants whose product requires a signed agreement; it is wired in per product, not per platform. The Customer Agreement consent recorded at Step 5 satisfies regulatory consent for retail savings on its own.

Behind the scenes (where in scope)

The channel calls POST /v1/documents/docusign/templates/send to dispatch a pre-populated template. The recipient signs in the e-signature provider's UI. A webhook posted by the provider is consumed by the platform and used to update an envelope tracking record. The channel polls GET /v1/documents/docusign/envelopes/status?caseId={caseId} until the envelope reaches a terminal state. The webhook is registered against the tenant during provisioning; no per-environment configuration is required from the bank.

Exit conditions

  • Pass. Envelope reaches signed state; signed document is stored in the Document Directory under the Customer Agreement record. Journey proceeds to Step 7.
  • Fail (recoverable). Envelope voided or expired. Channel may re-issue the template.
  • Fail (non-recoverable). Repeated decline or expiry leads to journey termination with the same configurable rejection message used at Step 4.

Step 7: Credential setup and account funding

The final screen captures a password and an initial deposit amount. The username is pre-populated, non-editable, and matches the email captured at Step 2. The password field follows the tenant's password policy. The deposit field validates as numeric, non-zero, positive, with no decimals and a maximum of 999,999,999 in the selected currency. Once the customer clicks Done after the Preview, the journey cannot be re-entered through the browser back button.

Fields captured

FieldTypeRequiredNotes

PasswordString (masked)YesEncrypted in transit and at rest. Show / hide toggle. Policy is tenant-configurable.

Behind the scenes

Password registration runs through Issued Device Administration: Initiate registers the password assignment, Update stores the password value. The deposit amount is captured by the channel and routed to the bank's payment provider integration. The reference implementation does not bundle a payment provider; see Partner Integration / Payments for the planned shape of that route. Where a payment provider is wired in, the resulting posting flows through the Position Keeping service domain on the activated account.

Exit conditions

  • Pass. Password registered. If a payment provider is wired in, deposit is posted and the account shows an opening balance. Journey complete.
  • Fail (recoverable). Invalid password or deposit value; customer corrects and resubmits.
  • Fail (non-recoverable, payment-side). Where a payment provider is wired in, a posting rejection by the ledger (insufficient general ledger balance, account in an invalid state, smart-contract validation failure) routes to the operations queue in Glass for manual intervention.

Saga compensation and error recovery

The seven-step journey is sequential by design, and the most dangerous compensation scenario is structurally prevented: IDV (Step 3) and KYC and AML screening (Step 4) both run before account creation (Step 5). If either fails, the journey halts at the Application Decision stage and no account is created in the ledger. The case "KYC fails after an account has already been provisioned" therefore cannot arise in this journey.

Within Step 5, the staged sub-flow gives the platform a natural compensation boundary. Accounts are created with ACCOUNT_STATUS_PENDING and only activated to ACCOUNT_STATUS_OPEN after parameters and consent have been recorded. If a sub-step fails, the account can remain in pending state and is reconciled by the operations workflow rather than left in an inconsistent activated state.

Current error handling guarantees

  • Errors propagate with full context preserved: the response includes the step that failed, a correlation identifier, and a typed error code.
  • Partial failures return an explicit error indicating incomplete state. Channels never receive a silent success on partial completion.
  • The pending-to-open staging in Step 5 prevents premature account activation when a downstream step has not yet confirmed success.

Target-state orchestration

Saga compensation is orchestrated by a workflow engine (Conductor) in the target architecture. Current production deployments use service-level retry and compensation patterns; the journey is moving toward a centrally orchestrated model that adds the following:

  • Reusable reversible and irreversible compensation templates, with embedded checkpointing, undo logic, fallback routing, and state recovery.
  • Human-in-the-loop workflows for manual intervention when automated compensation is insufficient (timeouts, ambiguous error states, fraud holds).
  • A centralised audit trail of compensation and recovery actions, queryable from Glass.
  • Compensation and escalation rules configurable at the workflow level rather than embedded in service code.

Party Lifecycle Management is the state anchor that ties all of this together. The lifecycle spec defines status tracking (In Progress, Completed, Failed) and the explicit pattern "close the lifecycle only when every downstream service domain has succeeded." That is a saga commit model and the workflow engine is the orchestrator that drives it.

Configurability reference

Three axes determine how much of the journey is configurable per tenant: the workflow itself (order and presence of steps), the screen layer (validation, error messages, document types), and the rules engine (decision thresholds and policies). Reviewing KYC results for a new customer

5 min readModule: Customer & Account Mgmt

Triage the daily KYC review queue, inspect IDV and AML evidence side by side, and disposition each case. This walkthrough covers the standard happy-path review for a new individual customer.

Steps

  1. Open the Task Management console. From the Glass home, select Task Management → Compliance · Pending review. The KYC review queue lists every party in pending_kyc state with completed IDV and AML evidence, sorted by SLA. Each row shows party reference, residency, risk band, and time in queue.
  2. Review the customer profile. Open a case to land on the KYC tab of the Customer & Account Management module. The IDV outcome (document type, liveness score, match confidence) and the AML screening result (PEP hits, sanctions hits, adverse media) are shown side by side, with the canonical party record and attached documents one click away.
  3. Make your decision. Choose Approve, Request more information, or Escalate to Supervisor. Approve transitions the party to active; Request more information opens a templated message to the channel; Escalate routes the case to a four-eyes queue. Every action, along with the rationale entered, is written to the audit trail.
Permission required. The Compliance Officer role is required to disposition KYC reviews. Approving a high-risk band customer additionally requires compliance.foureyes.
